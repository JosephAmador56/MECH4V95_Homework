1Why this problem

Small modular reactors (SMRs) are factory-built units of up to roughly 300 MWe (megawatts of electric power). Many near-term designs are light-water reactors, so their safety case rests on the same thermal limits as today's large pressurized water reactors (PWRs) and boiling water reactors (BWRs). Before a design can be licensed, deterministic safety analysis and probabilistic safety assessment (PSA) must show that, for every credible initiating event, the fuel stays within those limits.

For water-cooled cores the most important limit is the critical heat flux (CHF): the heat flux at which the heated wall loses stable contact with liquid and wall heat transfer collapses. Designers track the margin to it with the departure from nucleate boiling ratio (DNBR):

DNBR = q″CHF(local conditions) / q″actual

Safety analysis must show that the minimum DNBR stays above a limit that already includes the uncertainty of the CHF prediction. A more accurate CHF model with trustworthy uncertainty therefore translates directly into safety margin and operating flexibility.

Machine learning enters safety analysis in two places this project covers. First, as fast, accurate surrogate models with uncertainty for quantities like CHF, which best-estimate-plus-uncertainty analyses and dynamic PSA evaluate thousands of times. Second, as early identification of the initiating event (loss of coolant, steam generator tube rupture, steam line break, and so on), which determines the event tree and emergency procedure that apply.

About the data

We use two open datasets: the CHF database of the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), measured in heated round tubes, and NPPAD (Nuclear Power Plant Accident Data), a set of simulated accident transients for a large PWR. Natural-circulation SMR designs run at lower core mass flux than large PWRs, so you will report model performance separately in the low-mass-flux regime (G < 1000 kg m−2 s−1), where data are also sparser.

3CHF in ten minutes

Two ways to reach CHF: DNB and dryout

In flow boiling, CHF happens by two different mechanisms. At subcooled or low-quality conditions, bubbles crowd the wall until liquid can no longer reach it: departure from nucleate boiling (DNB), the limiting case in PWRs. At high quality, the flow is annular and CHF occurs when the thin liquid film on the wall evaporates away: dryout, the limiting case in BWRs. One model has to cover both regimes, which is why CHF is hard to fit with a single formula.

Equilibrium quality is defined from enthalpy, x = (h − hf) / hfg, where hf and hfg are the saturated-liquid enthalpy and latent heat at pressure P. Negative x means the bulk liquid is still below saturation (subcooled); 0 < x < 1 means a saturated two-phase mixture. It is calculated from an energy balance, not measured directly.

The heat balance

For a uniformly heated tube, energy conservation between the inlet and the outlet gives

xout = −Δhin/hfg + 4 q″ L / (G D hfg)   ⇔   q″ = G D (xout hfg + Δhin) / (4 L)

Worked check, row 1 (Lowdermilk 1958): D = 0.004 m, L = 0.396 m, P = 100 kPa (hfg ≈ 2257.5 kJ/kg), G = 77.5, x = 0.84, Δhin = 317 kJ/kg. The heat balance gives about 433 kW/m2; the measured CHF is 442 kW/m2. The 2% gap comes from rounding in the tabulated quality and the property data. Make this your first unit test.

Pitfall: leakage through the heat balance

If your inputs include x together with Δhin (or Tin), L, G, D and P, the target can be computed exactly from the inputs with the equation above. Such a model looks nearly perfect and predicts nothing. Use one of the valid formulations:

  • Local conditions (required in Tasks A–D): q″CHF = f(P, G, D, x). Add L only with a physical justification.
  • Inlet conditions (allowed for comparison in Task A): q″CHF = g(P, G, D, L, Δhin), with no x.

Trends a good model should reproduce

  • At fixed P, G and D, CHF decreases as local quality x increases. Near the onset of dryout the drop is often steep: the "limiting-quality" region.
  • CHF decreases with tube diameter; the lookup table below scales it as (D / 8 mm)−1/2.
  • CHF generally increases with mass flux at subcooled and low-quality conditions; at high quality the effect weakens or can reverse.
  • The pressure effect is non-monotonic, and CHF falls steeply as P approaches the critical pressure (22.06 MPa), where the latent heat vanishes.
  • With local conditions held fixed, heated length should matter little for long tubes. A strong dependence on L usually means the model is learning a data artifact.

The baseline every model must beat

The 2006 Groeneveld lookup table (LUT) is the industry reference. It tabulates CHF for an 8 mm tube at 24 pressures, 20 mass fluxes and 23 qualities, interpolates linearly in (P, G, x), and multiplies by (D / 8 mm)−1/2. It is essentially a smoothed, normalized data bank, which is to say a non-parametric regression model. On this database it reaches a mean predicted-to-measured (P/M) ratio of about 1.03 with a standard deviation of about 0.36 [1].

4Dataset 1: NRC CHF database

About 25,000 CHF measurements in vertical, uniformly heated, water-cooled round tubes, compiled from roughly 60 experimental programs over six decades. It is the database used to derive the 2006 LUT, published in digital form by the NRC in 2019 [2], and the reference dataset of the CHF benchmark on artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) run by the Nuclear Energy Agency of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD/NEA) [1]. Things to know before you model

  • Use a log target. CHF spans well over an order of magnitude and errors that matter are relative. Model ln q″CHF, or use a relative loss.
  • Negative inlet subcooling is not an error. In some sources Δhin < 0, meaning the flow enters already two-phase; Tin then equals the saturation temperature.
  • Heat balance. When the LUT was built, rows with heat-balance errors above 5% were screened out, so almost all rows should close within that band.
  • Data come in clusters. Each experiment contributes many closely spaced points from the same test section. Random splits put near-duplicates in train and test, which flatters every model. That is why Part 0 also defines a leave-sources-out split.
  • Coverage is uneven. There is no data above 16 mm diameter, and very low mass flux and near-critical pressure are sparse. Expect larger errors there and say so.

Step 1. Load and check

Run Part 1 of the starter notebook, which builds and loads chf_nrc.csv. Confirm the row count (about 24.6k), the units, and that there are no missing values. Row 1 should be Number 1, D = 0.004 m, CHF = 442 kW/m2, Lowdermilk 1958.

Step 2. Heat-balance check

Recompute CHF from the heat balance for every row and plot the ratio to the measured value. Report the fraction within ±5%. Use iapws or CoolProp for water properties.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from iapws import IAPWS97          # pip install iapws

df = pd.read_csv("chf_nrc.csv")

def h_fg(P_kPa):                    # latent heat, kJ/kg
    P = P_kPa / 1000.0               # MPa
    return IAPWS97(P=P, x=1).h - IAPWS97(P=P, x=0).h

hfg_map = {p: h_fg(p) for p in df["P_kPa"].unique()}
hfg = df["P_kPa"].map(hfg_map)
q_hb = df.G_kgm2s * df.D_m * (df.X_chf * hfg + df.DHin_kJkg) / (4 * df.L_m)
ratio = q_hb / df.CHF_kWm2          # row 1 should give about 0.98

Step 5. Metrics

Following the OECD/NEA benchmark [1], compute P/Mi = ŷi / yi in kW/m2, not in log space. The function returns the mean of P/M (the bias, ideally 1), the standard deviation of P/M (the scatter, your headline number), the root mean square percentage error (RMSPE), the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and the Q2 error (EQ2, squared error divided by the variance of the measurements):

def chf_metrics(y_true, y_pred):
    pm, rel = y_pred / y_true, y_pred / y_true - 1.0
    return {"mean_PM": pm.mean(),                       # bias, ideal 1
            "std_PM":  pm.std(ddof=1),                  # scatter, the headline number
            "RMSPE_%": 100 * np.sqrt(np.mean(rel**2)),
            "MAPE_%":  100 * np.mean(np.abs(rel)),
            "EQ2":     np.sum((y_pred - y_true)**2) / np.sum((y_true - y_true.mean())**2)}

Step 6. Common baseline

Fit ordinary least squares (OLS) on (P, G, D, x) to ln CHF and report all metrics on the S1 test set. Everyone reports this number; it calibrates every gain you claim later.

Reference anchors

LUT on the full database: mean P/M ≈ 1.03, std P/M ≈ 0.36. Example neural network in the OECD/NEA specification (inputs P, G, D, L, x; random 80/20 split): test mean P/M ≈ 1.01, std P/M ≈ 0.12, RMSPE ≈ 13% [1]. A well-tuned model on S1 should land near the second set of numbers. S2 will be noticeably worse; explaining why is part of the work.

6Dataset 2: NPPAD accident transients

NPPAD [5] contains accident simulations for a generic three-loop PWR at full power, generated with the PCTRAN (Personal Computer Transient Analyzer) simulator. Each run is a multivariate time series of 96 plant variables plus time, sampled every 10 s. The accident is initiated at t = 20 s and runs last about 4000 s on average. Most accident types are simulated over a sweep of severities (for example, break size), and the file name is the severity value.

Pitfall: oracle variables

Some simulator outputs measure the accident itself: WLR and HLW (RCS leak flow and enthalpy), WBK, MBK, EBK (break flow and its integrals), WTRA/WTRB (tube leak flow per steam generator) and WFLB (feedwater-line break flow). Real plants do not instrument these directly, and with them classification becomes trivial. Report results both with all variables and with this list removed ("realistic instrumentation").

C

Physics-informed learning with little data

Topics
Physics-informed ML: structural priors, constraint losses, hybrid models
Data
CHF, Part 0 splits including S3
Map
(P, G, D, x) → q″CHF that respects known physics

Core

  • C1Unconstrained baseline. An MLP on (P, G, D, x) → ln CHF, tuned on S1 validation.
  • C2Hard structural prior. Build the diameter scaling into the architecture: q″CHF = (D / 0.008 m)−1/2 · exp(fθ(P, G, x)).
  • C3Soft constraint loss. Add a penalty λ · mean[ReLU(∂q″/∂x)]2 at collocation points sampled uniformly over the input domain, including where there is no data. Compute the derivative with autograd. This is the idea behind physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), applied to a known inequality instead of the residual of a partial differential equation (PDE). Choose λ on validation and report the constraint-violation rate on a dense grid.
  • C4Data-scarcity study. Train C1, C2 and C3 on 2%, 5%, 10%, 25% and 100% of S1 train (at least three seeds each). Plot S1 test and S3 error against training fraction, and show slices 1, 7 and 9 for the 5% case.

Extensions (do at least two)

  • Residual learning on a prior model [4]: ln q″ = ln q″prior + rθ, with the 2006 LUT values from [3] (if available) or a published correlation as the prior.
  • Heat-balance-consistent prediction: given inlet conditions, solve q″ = fθ(P, G, D, xout(q″)) by fixed-point iteration, and compare with a direct inlet-conditions model.
  • Monotonicity by construction (for example, monotone network layers) against the soft penalty.
  • A λ sweep: the accuracy–physics trade-off curve, and where it stops paying off.

Start here

Raissi et al. [9] for PINNs; Zhao et al. [4] for physics-informed CHF prediction with a prior model. A typical collocation domain: P 0.1–20 MPa, G 50–8000, D 2–16 mm, x −0.5 to 1.0.

  • Severity regression, for example LOCA break size from the first 60 s, with uncertainty [12].
  • DNBR early warning: from the first 60 s, forecast the minimum DNBR over the next 300 s and flag runs that cross a threshold you justify. This links Task E to the CHF tasks.
  • Robustness: test on the accidents during power ramps in Variable_Power_Data/, which the model never saw.
  • Unknown-accident detection: hold out one class entirely and use predictive entropy, MC dropout or ensembles to flag it.
  • Interpretability: which signals drive each decision, and do they make engineering sense?

Start here

Qi et al. [5] and the repository's Data Processing.py; PyTorch Conv1d, LSTM, GRU. Accuracy alone hides class confusion, so lead with macro-F1 and confusion matrices.

8Deliverables

  • Report (PDF). At most 10 pages, excluding references and appendices. Suggested structure: problem and formulation, data and splits, methods, results, discussion (physics, failure modes, what this means for safety margins), limitations.
  • Code. A repository or zip with a README that states how to reproduce every table and figure, a requirements.txt or environment.yml, and fixed random seeds. Notebooks are fine if they run top to bottom.
  • Prediction files. For Tasks A–D, pred_<task>_S1test.csv with columns Number, y_true, y_pred (kW/m2), plus lower_95 and upper_95 for Task B. For Task E, one row per test run with true label, predicted label and class probabilities at W = 60 s. We will recompute your metrics from these.
  • Statement of sources. One paragraph listing libraries, any external code you adapted (with links), and any AI tools you used and how.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Tuning on the test set, or reporting the best of many test-set runs.
  • Comparing models trained on different splits or different input sets.
  • Fitting CHF in linear space with MSE, then wondering why low-CHF points have huge relative errors.
  • For NPPAD, splitting windows instead of runs, or normalizing with statistics from the whole dataset.
  • Accuracy numbers with no picture of physical behavior: always show slices (CHF) or confusion matrices (NPPAD).

Practical notes

  • Libraries: numpy, pandas, scikit-learn, PyTorch, GPyTorch, iapws or CoolProp, matplotlib. Optional: Optuna, SHAP.
  • Compute: an MLP on 20k rows trains in minutes on a CPU. Exact GPs become slow beyond about 5000 points; that is the point of B2. Small tabular diffusion models train on a CPU in under an hour.

AAppendix: build chf_nrc.csv from the NRC PDF

The NRC data file is a text-based PDF table with columns Number, Data, D, L, P, G, Xchf, DHin, CHF, Tin, Reference. The starter notebook already runs this extraction; the script is here for reference. The line parser has been tested on rows from several sources.

# pip install pdfplumber pandas
import re, pdfplumber, pandas as pd

NUM = re.compile(r"^-?(\d+\.?\d*|\.\d+)([eE][-+]?\d+)?$")
COLS = ["Number", "DataID", "D_m", "L_m", "P_kPa", "G_kgm2s",
        "X_chf", "DHin_kJkg", "CHF_kWm2", "Tin_C", "Reference"]

def parse_line(line):
    t = line.split()
    if len(t) < 10 or not t[0].isdigit():
        return None                          # header, units line, footer
    vals = t[2:10]
    if not all(NUM.match(v) for v in vals):
        return None
    return [int(t[0]), t[1], *map(float, vals), " ".join(t[10:]).lstrip("*")]

rows = []
with pdfplumber.open("ML22264A009.pdf") as pdf:
    for page in pdf.pages:
        for line in (page.extract_text() or "").splitlines():
            r = parse_line(line)
            if r:
                rows.append(r)

df = (pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=COLS)
        .drop_duplicates("Number").sort_values("Number"))
print(len(df))   # expect about 24.6k (OECD/NEA version: 24,579)
df.to_csv("chf_nrc.csv", index=False)

Alternative without Python PDF libraries: pdftotext -layout ML22264A009.pdf chf.txt, then apply parse_line to each line. If your row count is far from 24.6k, inspect lines that the parser rejected.

BAppendix: load NPPAD and split by run

from pathlib import Path
import pandas as pd

ROOT = Path("NuclearPowerPlantAccidentData/Operation_csv_data")
CLASSES = ["LOCA", "LOCAC", "SLBIC", "SLBOC", "SGATR", "SGBTR",
           "RW", "RI", "FLB", "MD", "LR", "LLB"]
ORACLE = ["WLR", "HLW", "WBK", "MBK", "EBK", "WTRA", "WTRB", "WFLB"]

runs = []
for c in CLASSES:
    for f in (ROOT / c).glob("*.csv"):
        try:
            sev = float(f.stem)
        except ValueError:
            continue
        runs.append({"label": c, "severity": sev,
                     "df": pd.read_csv(f).sort_values("TIME")})

# variables present in every run (drops the few extra columns some files carry)
VARS = sorted(set.intersection(*(set(r["df"].columns) for r in runs)) - {"TIME"})
VARS_REAL = [v for v in VARS if v not in ORACLE]

# run-level split: within each class, every 5th run (by severity) to test
for c in CLASSES:
    cls = sorted((r for r in runs if r["label"] == c), key=lambda r: r["severity"])
    for k, r in enumerate(cls):
        r["split"] = "test" if k % 5 == 0 else ("val" if k % 5 == 1 else "train")

def window(df, W_s, cols, t0=20.0):
    seg = df[(df.TIME >= t0) & (df.TIME < t0 + W_s)]
    return seg[cols].to_numpy()             # shape (about W_s / 10, n_vars)
